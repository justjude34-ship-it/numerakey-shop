Question 1 of 18 · Idealization & love-bombing

At the start (or after conflict), they flooded you with praise, gifts, or intense attention that felt almost too good to be true.

Love-bombing can create a powerful bond quickly. The intensity is the point — it can make later coldness or criticism feel like a loss of something precious you must earn back.

Question 2 of 18 · Idealization & love-bombing

They talked about a perfect shared future (moving in, marriage, big plans) very early, before you really knew each other.

Future-faking paints a beautiful picture that may never be followed through. It can keep you invested in a story rather than the present reality.

Question 3 of 18 · Gaslighting & reality distortion

They deny saying or doing things you clearly remember — or insist you are “too sensitive,” “crazy,” or “remembering wrong.”

Gaslighting erodes trust in yourself. Over time you may second-guess every feeling. Your memory and feelings are still valid, even when someone works hard to rewrite them.

Question 4 of 18 · Gaslighting & reality distortion

After arguments, you often end up apologizing for something you didn’t do — or feel confused about what actually happened.

When conflict consistently leaves you disoriented or blamed, it may be a pattern of deflection rather than repair. Healthy repair feels clarifying, not foggy.

Question 5 of 18 · Devaluation & criticism

Compliments or affection alternate with put-downs, mockery, or cold silence — sometimes without clear reason.

The idealize–devalue cycle keeps you chasing the “good” version of them. Intermittent warmth is powerfully reinforcing; you deserve steadier respect.

Question 6 of 18 · Devaluation & criticism

They criticize your appearance, intelligence, friends, family, or achievements in ways that leave you smaller.

Chronic criticism isn’t “honesty” or “helping you grow.” It’s a way of keeping you off-balance. Kind feedback builds up; this kind of talk wears down.

Question 7 of 18 · Control & isolation

They monitor your phone, messages, location, spending, or social life — or get angry when you spend time with others.

Control often disguises itself as protection or love. Autonomy and privacy are normal in healthy relationships.

Question 8 of 18 · Control & isolation

You feel you must manage their moods — walking on eggshells to avoid anger, sulking, or punishment.

Hypervigilance is exhausting. You are not responsible for regulating another adult’s every emotion, especially through fear of consequences.

Question 9 of 18 · Empathy & accountability

When you share hurt feelings, they dismiss, mock, or turn the conversation back to how hard things are for them.

Lack of empathy leaves wounds unattended. Being heard and taken seriously is a baseline of care, not a luxury.

Question 10 of 18 · Empathy & accountability

They rarely take real responsibility — blame shifts to you, circumstances, or “jokes,” with little lasting change.

Accountability means owning impact and changing behaviour. Empty apologies without change keep the cycle spinning.

Question 11 of 18 · How you feel

You feel anxious, drained, or “not yourself” more often around them than with other people in your life.

Your nervous system notices patterns before your mind fully names them. Persistent dread or depletion is meaningful information.

Question 12 of 18 · How you feel

You second-guess your worth, needs, or reality more than you used to — or feel lonely even when you’re together.

Emotional loneliness inside a relationship is a deep form of pain. Reconnecting with your own voice is a brave, healthy step.

Question 13 of 18 · Gaslighting & reality distortion

They use private information you shared in trust against you later — during fights or to humiliate you.

Weaponizing vulnerability breaks safety. Trust thrives when soft spots are protected, not stored as ammunition.

Question 14 of 18 · Control & isolation

They make big decisions that affect you without real input — or punish you (silent treatment, threats, guilt) if you disagree.

Partnership includes mutual say. Coercion and punishment for differing opinions are not compromise.

Question 15 of 18 · Empathy & accountability

They show little curiosity about your inner world, yet expect deep attention to theirs.

One-way emotional labour is depleting. Reciprocal care means both people matter — including you.

Question 16 of 18 · Devaluation & criticism

Publicly they may seem charming; privately you experience a harsher, colder, or more critical version.

A large gap between public image and private behaviour can leave you feeling crazy or isolated — “no one else sees this.” You are not imagining it.

Question 17 of 18 · Idealization & love-bombing

After you try to leave or set a boundary, they become unusually loving, apologetic, or desperate — until things settle, then the old patterns return.

Hoovering (pulling you back in) often resets the cycle rather than ending it. Lasting change shows up in consistent behaviour over time, not crisis romance.

Question 18 of 18 · How you feel

You feel guilty for needing space, boundaries, or support — as if your needs are selfish or “too much.”

Needs for safety, respect, and rest are human. Guilt about having needs is often trained, not innate. You are allowed to take up space.

5-4-3-2-1

After an argument, a spiral, or feeling far away.

5 things you can see. Colours, shapes, light.
4 things you can feel. Feet, fabric, temperature.
3 sounds. Near or far, including your breath.
2 scents, or imagine two safe ones.
1 taste, or a slow sip of water.

Box breathing

For a racing heart, or before a hard conversation. In for 4, hold 4, out for 4, hold 4. Four rounds is enough.
