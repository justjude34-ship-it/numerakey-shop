Lesson 1 Done

The fear is the first obstacle

Most people think AI is magic, or something only tech people can use. So they never open it. It is a chat window on your phone. You type what you want in ordinary English. It answers. There is no spell, and there is no coding for you to learn.

The AI writes the code. Your job is the idea, and saying it clearly. Once you start, it feels straightforward. The hard reputation is mostly the fear of a thing you have not tried.

Before any tool, write the app in two or three sentences, the way you would tell a friend. Not a plan. Not a feature list. Who it is for, what they do, what they get.

Too vague

An app that helps people with stuff.

Clear enough to build

An app that helps busy parents plan weekly dinners. They tap a button, it suggests five meals from ingredients they already have, and they can save favourites.

The clear one names the person, the action, and the result. That is enough for an AI to start.

Your turn

Write your idea in two or three sentences. If you cannot name who it is for and what it does in one breath, it is not ready yet.

Lesson 2 Done

Pick an AI and type something

The names sound like they belong to someone else. They do not. ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok are chat windows. You type. They answer. That is the whole interface. Any of them can help you build an app.

They differ in personality, not in magic. ChatGPT is the most common, and has the most add-ons. Claude is often better at long, careful writing. Grok is direct, and will push back if the idea has holes. For this lesson, none of that matters. Open one.

Paste this
I want to build an app that helps busy parents plan weekly dinners. What questions should you ask me before we start?

Read the answer. It will ask things you had not thought of. That is the point. It is a thinking partner, not a vending machine. The better you answer, the better the app gets.

Your turn

Which AI did you open, and what did it ask you that you had not thought of?

Lesson 3 Done

Ask well

The AI is only as good as what you give it. A vague prompt gets a vague app. “Make me an app” is a weak prompt. A strong one names the person, the action, and the result, then adds limits: what it should look like, what it should not do, and what the first version should leave out.

First versions should be small. One job, done clearly, beats ten half-finished ones. You can always add more later. That is lesson four.

A strong prompt looks like this
Build a small app for busy parents planning weekly dinners. They tap one button and get five meals based on ingredients they type in. They can save favourites. Keep it calm, with big text and not much on the screen. Do not add accounts, shopping, or a calendar. First version only: suggest meals and save favourites.

Your turn

Expand your two sentences. Who it is for. What they do. What they get. What it should look like. What the first version should skip.

Lesson 4 Done

The first version will be rough

You can get something on screen very fast if you do not care how good it is. Making it good takes another kind of work. You build it, you try it, you say exactly what is wrong, and you build again. That loop is called iteration. It is the real work.

A rough first version is not failure. It is the start. People who want it really good stay in the loop. People who only wanted to see if it was possible can stop. Both are allowed. Know which one you are doing.

Paste this after you try it
Fix only these three things. Do not redesign the rest. 1. … 2. … 3. …

Your turn

Use what it built for five minutes, the way a real person would. Write three things that annoyed you. Paste them back. Do that three times.

Lesson 5 Done

Decide what it is for

This is the fork, and it depends on their intention — yours, in this case.

  • Personal use. It only has to work for you. No polish required.
  • Sharing. Someone else has to understand it without you standing there explaining.
  • Selling. It has to solve a real problem for someone who would pay. It needs a name, and a place to live.

The answer decides how much effort the app deserves. A personal tool can stay rough. A thing you sell cannot. Do not polish a private tool as if it were a product, and do not ship a product that only makes sense to you.

Your turn

One sentence on which of the three is yours. One sentence on what that means for how far you take it.

Lesson 6 Done

When you get stuck

You will get stuck. That is the normal middle of the loop, not a sign you failed.

Do not re-explain the whole app. Copy the exact words on the screen, or the last thing the AI said, and paste them back. A summary throws the clue away. The precise error is the whole clue.

Paste this with the exact words
This is what happened. Fix only this.

If it goes in circles, start a fresh chat. Paste your original idea, plus what already works, and ask it to continue from there. You are not starting over. You are giving it a clean page.

Your turn

Next time it breaks, paste the exact words here so you have them, then send them back.

Lesson 7 Done

One file, on the phone, offline

Ask the AI to build the whole app as a single HTML file. You save it. You open it in any browser. That is an app. No account, no server, no monthly fee, and no coding from you.

It can run offline. Once the file is on the device, the browser does not need the internet to open it. This course is built the same way.

If you want an icon on the home screen, full screen, with no browser bar, ask for a PWA — a progressive web app. It is still just a page. People install it from a link. They tap Install, or Add to Home Screen, and it lands on the phone.

App stores

Google Play wants a $25 developer account, a signed build, and a review that can take days before anyone can install it. Apple’s store is $99 a year, plus stricter rules. Someone else decides if your app is allowed.

A single file, or a PWA

You share a link. They tap install. It sits on the home screen, opens like an app, and can run offline. No fee, no waiting, no gatekeeper.

The finish-line prompt
Build this as one HTML file I can save and open in a browser. No build step, no accounts, no external libraries. It should work offline. Make it installable on a phone home screen. Use plain language. Here is the app: [paste your idea]

That is the whole course. From a fear you had not named, to a working app in your pocket. The coding was never your job.
