    The ideas underneath everything

    Chi, the five elements,
    yin and yang.

    Feng shui means wind and water — the two things that move. Strip away the folklore and it's a way of noticing how a room makes you feel, then adjusting it on purpose.

    氣 Chi — the flow

    Chi is the energy that moves through a space. The practical version: imagine water poured in your front door. Where would it rush straight through? Where would it pool and go stagnant? Where would it snag on clutter?

    You want it to meander — entering easily, curving through rooms, never blasting straight out the back window. Blocked doorways, dead corners and long uninterrupted corridors are the usual culprits.

    陰陽 Yin and yang — the balance

    Every space holds two qualities. Yin is quiet, soft, dark, still — good for bedrooms and reading corners. Yang is bright, hard, loud, active — good for kitchens, offices and entryways.

    Neither is better. Trouble comes from too much of one: a bedroom with glaring light and a TV can't hold sleep, and a home office in a dim, soft, cave-like room can't hold focus.

    The five elements

    Five moods, each with its own colours, shapes and materials. Most rooms lean too far into one. The fix is usually adding a little of what's missing, not redecorating.

    How the elements act on each other

    This is the part most people skip, and it's the part that makes feng shui usable. Two cycles: one where each element feeds the next, one where each element keeps another in check. Tap any element to trace both.

    Yang — activate

    • Daylight and reflection
    • Straight lines, hard surfaces
    • Bright, saturated colours
    • Sound and movement
    • Kitchens, offices, hallways
